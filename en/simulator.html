Ombra Prime 16.9 km · 100,000 inhabitants

A world that writes itself, driven by real measurements.

Ombra Prime Simulator reads sensors in the physical world — the power use of a house, the response time of a network, the dust in the air, air pressure, humidity, movement in a room, whatever moves outside a kitchen window — and translates them into events aboard a generation ship. An AI writes the scenes. Nobody types in what is supposed to happen.

What the chronicle said

“My sister has a Vitalis organ, and she woke at four and said it was creaking. I said organs don’t creak. She said I should feel it for myself, and I felt nothing — but she sat up until the shift began all the same.” Stifur Mandrattur, technician · sector 03

The idea

The ship is given. The story is not.

Eight sectors and thirteen main characters are fixed and never change. Everything else — workshops, corridors, minor characters, conflicts — grows out of the scenes as they are written.

The Chronicler invents a growing ring in one scene. Another agent reads the scene, picks out the place and proposes it. A third approves it and writes it into canon. The next scene in the same sector gets to see that the growing ring exists, and can use it again.

That is how the world grows by itself, without anyone losing the thread — and without anyone having to remember what has already happened.

The chain

From a number to a scene, in six steps.

Each link has its own job and leaves behind something the next one can use. Everything is kept, in order, so that any sentence in the chronicle can be traced back to the reading that caused it.

Reality is measured

A sensor reads something physical. Network response against a public host, the power meter in the house, a Raspberry Pi 5 with a Sense HAT and a particle sensor — air pressure, humidity, dust — a webcam looking out through a kitchen window at a bird table, and a small ESP32 board that senses movement in the room without a camera, from how the wifi waves bounce. The raw value means nothing in itself — what counts is the deviation from a moving baseline. The air aboard follows the dust and the hull stress a falling air pressure. But most gauges do not read a sensor straight off; they read a derived value: how far the number stands from its own normal, how fast it changes, the imbalance between the three phases of the power, what share of the day something has moved. The water balance aboard follows how evenly the power runs, not the humidity. The camera is the ship’s lookout: when something comes to the bird table, something is outside the hull. The image is not kept and never published. A reviewer sees a small crop around the movement and says what it was. If it recognises a deer, that is a comet; a squirrel is an object caught in the ship’s wake; a crow is an unknown probe. If it cannot tell, it stays a mystery, and a person in the picture never becomes anything at all. The movement is the pulse of the city: Metropolis sleeps when the house sleeps, wakes when it wakes, and fills with people when there is a party. The power, which switches on and off around the clock, is the work in The Crypt, where Oris and her machines build without anyone aboard knowing what. Once enough work has gathered they have finished something, and the shape of the power draw decides what: steady running makes something small and fine, heavy loads something large. The people aboard only notice the traces. And if the simulator stands still, that becomes an event too: days missing from the ship’s log.

The director decides whether it becomes a scene

Most omens are stopped, and that is the point. Four gates: severity, rest between scenes, daily budget, zone focus. Omens that pass land in a waiting basket and compete with each other — the dark must not drown in the bright, and what just became a scene gets a minus. The winner goes to one of the active threads, at least six at a time, which are born and die in step with the scenes rather than the clock, and a weighted wheel draws which sector the scene lands in.

The world grows

An archivist reads the scene and proposes what is new. A keeper decides what becomes true, merges duplicates and rejects what breaks the laws of the world. Only then does it really exist. The archivist also decides whether the scene changed something that lasts: something broke, someone moved, a decision was made. Then the sector gets a line in its memory, and the next scene there builds on it.

The ship

Eight sectors, top to bottom.

Ombra Prime is organised vertically. Each sector has a sheet: what it is, what is done there, what never belongs there, who gets in, how bright it is and how it feels for a person to stand there. Whoever writes a scene gets only the sheet for the sector the wheel has drawn, so that nothing else rubs off. That is why a scene in the Hangar tastes of metal and one in The Crypt tastes of ozone. Everyone aboard has a home sector. Away from it you are a visitor, and only Oris is everywhere.

The world model is a switch. It can run entirely locally on a graphics card — no API key, no cost per scene — or on a model over the network. Since November 2026 the whole simulator lives on a computer of its own that runs around the clock, and the world’s agents are on Claude Haiku. What is allowed to happen is decided by code either way.

Anything that costs money per run always has a ceiling. A radio show is read aloud only when a person presses a button, and the price is on the button first. The songs are the exception: those that make it onto the week’s chart are recorded by themselves, at most two new ones a week and under a daily ceiling. The music has moved twice: the provider pulled it from its API in August, it ran for a while on a local graphics card, and since November the same model runs at fal.ai, paid per song.

What prevents drift

A world that writes itself has to be kept from contradicting itself.

That is the hard part. A model allowed to write freely for months will sooner or later let a dead person speak, move a sealed door or invent a ninth sector. Seven things stand in the way.

The laws

The world’s physics are read-only

A handful of rules that no agent may break, and that no agent can change. They go into every prompt.

The keeper

Only one instance writes canon

The agents propose, never more. The Keeper decides, merges duplicates and requires something to be mentioned twice before it becomes true. What the creator himself has written is locked: no agent may change it. New people get a first and a last name, and the first name points to them — or to nobody, if two share it.

The hierarchy

Everything knows where it belongs

A new place must be anchored in one of the eight sectors. If it cannot be, the proposal is dropped rather than becoming a homeless string.

The reviewer

Repetition raises the alarm

Mechanical checks measure title templates, verbal tics, opening forms and zone bias. If the Chronicler writes the same scene again, it shows.

Home

Everyone has a home sector

Each person belongs to one sector and may visit certain others. The cast follows it: two places in every scene go to those who live there, and someone who may not be in the sector is left out. That is how each sector becomes a world of its own, with its own people.

Memory

What happened has consequences

Each sector remembers at most ten larger things that happened there and still hold. The lines go along to the next scene in the sector. Something left unresolved comes back after a while as a later scene’s assignment.

Reuse

The world counts what it returns to

After every scene the people, places and objects it mentioned are counted. Whoever has rested longest is first in line, so that the world uses what it already has instead of only inventing more.

The face to the outside

Mara Rox broadcasts what has happened.

The ship’s news anchor writes a broadcast script when enough has gathered — not by the clock, but by content. The script knows which scenes it has already covered, so the same event is never broadcast twice.

The script is written to be read aloud. Picture directions are marked and not read. At the end of every script is a heading that never goes on air: “What she did not broadcast” — what she knows but chose to leave out.

Outputs

The world grows for its own sake. What comes out of it should be usable.

A broadcast to record, a book to read, an episode to cut. That is why nothing leaves the system as a document someone has to interpret — it leaves as a package: a folder where every file has a job, and a manifest that says which.

paket.jsonmanifestWhat the package contains and what each part is for. The only thing that has to be read.

underlag.mdsourcesWhat Mara got to read before she wrote, so that every claim can be traced.
ljud/tal.wavreadingThe speech, already read aloud. Every broadcast becomes two packages: one English and one Swedish.
internt.mdsecretWhat Mara knew and chose to leave out. May never leave the package.

The last row is no courtesy. The difference between what the anchor knows and what she broadcasts is her whole character — if it leaks, the broadcast is ruined. That is why the package builder compares every six-word sequence in the secret with everything going out, and refuses to write the package if anything recurs. A rule that only lives in an instruction is a hope.

The book

The Chronicler stands in the room. The Novelist knows how it turned out.

When enough scenes have gathered, a second writer comes in and writes a chapter about what happened. It reads exactly the same scenes that are already written — but it writes backwards, and that is the whole reason it exists.

The chronicle · in the moment

“Dr. Evelyn Saros drew her hand through the cool, damp air of the control room and felt an unusual dryness bite at her skin.”

does not know what comes next
The novel · in hindsight

“They thought it was a mechanical problem, a matter of logistics and spare parts. They could not have been more wrong.”

knows exactly what comes next

The sentence on the right can only be written by someone who has already seen the ending. That is what the novel adds: it gets to tie things together, foreshadow and reweigh — to say that a passing detail turned out to decide everything. The chapters are written one at a time and never touched again, and between them the author carries a short summary, because the whole book cannot possibly fit in its head.

But nothing goes back. The novel reads the world and never writes it. Were it allowed to invent on top of the Chronicler’s inventions, the third generation would be wholly out of touch with the sensors — and the novel’s inventions are claims about why something happened, the hardest of all to take back.

The radio

Ombra Prime Radio — a glimpse into the world.

The ship already had two ways of telling about itself, and both are about the main characters — they are the ones in the scenes. The radio does the opposite. It is the only format where someone with no part in the plot gets to talk for nine minutes.

Lisa Lusis reads what the news anchor reported, has an opinion about it, takes a call from someone working the night shift, plays two songs from the chart and reads out the positions. In between run adverts for things that actually exist aboard. Everything she says comes from canon — and nothing she says is written back.

But she is no longer alone on air. Three more voices have segments of their own, with their own agents, their own voices and their own background: a logbook keeper who reads the latest novel chapter verbatim, a confessor who holds a verse of the ship’s scripture up against how the week has felt, and a stores clerk who counts what was not said. A show is now just under half an hour.

A show always takes the latest — Mara’s latest broadcast, the latest scenes, the novel’s latest chapter, the chart as it stands. And a new one is made only when there is something new to tell: new news and a new chapter. The chart is the week’s, and the same in all of the week’s shows. It is never a rerun. The button that makes the show lights up only then, with the price on it.

Lisa, Haldis and Ivo have voices at ElevenLabs. Lisa’s can sigh, laugh quietly and pause where the script says so; the other two read more calmly, like people not used to a microphone. The reader, the callers and the adverts have MiniMax voices, and a man who calls in gets a man’s voice.

The voices

The interesting thing about the three new ones is what they may not see.

A character who keeps a secret does not do so because it has been told to. A prompt is a hope. The rule has to lie in what material the voice gets — so that there is nothing to reveal even if it wanted to.

The voiceGets to seeNever gets to see

The last two mirror each other, and that is no accident. She gets the shape of the mood without the cause; he gets the shape of the hole without the contents. So he leaks nothing — he notices that something is missing, is right that the gap exists and wrong about what lies in it.

The rumours in the stream carry a field saying what the teller exaggerates. He never sees it. If he did, he would either correct himself — and then he is no whistleblower — or say it anyway, and then he is a liar. A man who is wrong and believes he is right is better than both. That the listener has followed the world and knows better is not a problem to solve; it is what makes the segment worth hearing.

On top of the material sit mechanical checks. If the count names a person from canon, the whole segment is dropped — a name turns a gap into an accusation. And if the anchor held nothing back, there is no hole, so the segment is dropped then too.

The sound

Every voice has its own room. The reader has silence.

A bed is not a song. It should not be heard as music, it should be heard as the room having a colour — and when you notice it is there, it is too loud. With four people on air, the same loop under all four made them the same programme with different readers. The bed is the cheapest way to say the room has changed: it is heard before the listener has caught a single word.

The station has its drone. The confessor has ringing metal that rings out in the pauses instead of lying under the speech. The counter has a dry ticking pulse. And the reader has nothing at all — the music ends when he starts reading and comes back when he is done. The contrast is the effect: a bed under a novel chapter makes it a radio play, no bed makes it a man reading aloud in a quiet room.

Two things turned up on the way. The provider’s instrumental flag is not enough — it makes no sung words, but happily breathing tones that under a speaking voice sound like someone standing in the room. The ban therefore lives in the code and is added to every bed prompt, because it is not a property of one particular bed but of what a bed is. And silence has to be told apart from a missing bed: a role without a background gets the station’s drone, while the reader has chosen silence.

Speech is reused whenever it can be. This change — a new bed under the confessor, silence under the reader — cost zero speech synthesis: eleven of eleven segments used speech that was already read. Only the background was redone.

The music

A song has to exist in the world before it may be played.

A song that is only an audio file turns the radio into a jukebox. A song that is an entity in canon turns it into something else: then the host can tell who wrote it, where aboard they live, and that it has been on the chart for three weeks and is still climbing.

The songs are objects, the artists are people, and both are created on the simulator side — not in the radio. The ship should be able to have a music scene even if the channel is never started. The lyrics come from the world: one of the first is about a kitchen worker who notices that her own portions are no longer being logged and has to decide whether to report it.

It opens a vein of world-building nothing else in the project reaches. Musicians are exactly the kind of background figures who otherwise never get room — and a song about the life flow faltering, written by someone who lives under the dome, says more about the ship than yet another report about the same thing. It is how the people aboard process what happens.

And the chart moves for a reason

Not by chance, and not by plays — the latter would be circular, since the chart decides what is played. It is the world. Every scene since the last chart is a ballot: the place it happens in, the thread it belongs to and its keynote. A song climbs when what happens aboard resembles what the song is about.

All songs compete, recorded or not. Every week new songs are written about the week’s threads, as text. A new song has to spend a week bubbling under before it can enter, and only those that enter are recorded. The style is drawn with a weighted wheel, built like the sector wheel: the sector, the thread’s tone and how crowded a style already is. That is why the week’s songs sound different, and an artist keeps their style.

WeightWhat it measures
inertiawhere the song was last week, as ballast. Without it the chart is reshuffled every week and no song has time to become well known.
resonancedoes the song’s keynote match the week’s? Does the artist live where something happened?
fatigueweeks on the chart pull it down. Slowly, but relentlessly — nothing stays forever.
debutwhere a song from outside the chart starts. Low enough that a number one is not pushed out in a week.

Four numbers in a config file and no hidden logic. A chart that moves for reasons nobody can explain is a chart nobody can trust — and the host reads it out as if it meant something. The chart runs Monday to Sunday, like a real chart. Next week’s is counted on the Sunday and takes effect on the Monday. If the world has stood still, it is a quiet week where inertia and fatigue decide.

The first chart could be derived line by line: number one was a song that both shared the week’s keynote and had an artist living where the scenes took place. Three songs shared only the keynote. Last came the only bright song, in a week that was ambiguous.

The boundary

The radio reads the world. The world does not know the radio exists.

Everything the channel knows comes through a single file, and it comes as frozen data classes — not as database rows. It costs a few lines of translation per question and gives three things: a window that can be closed if the simulator changes, a broadcast that cannot lie by mistake, and the possibility of running the whole radio with no world at all.

The rule is not an ambition. A command reads the imports and fails every file that reaches outside the module — in both directions. It has failed four files during the build, most recently a music driver that wanted to measure audio length with the radio’s tools.

The engine must run without the radio. Always.

One show picked a background figure as caller. The same person happened to be number one on the chart — and the host made the connection herself, without being told to:

“This is a song by someone who called in just now. Pell Ohnstad, The Pearl. They called to talk about the life flow faltering, and they write songs down by the lower markets. This one is called Three Spoons.”

No rule says a caller may be an artist · it follows from both drawing on the same canon
Live

The world is broadcast while it is being written.

The radio is broadcast live on YouTube, on the channel Ombra Prime. The latest show runs until a new one exists, and the chart plays in the broadcast.

The broadcast is run by Oris — an AI agent who lives on the same computer as the simulator and bears the name of the ship’s mind. She only reads: the show and the chart are fetched where the simulator puts them, and nothing in the broadcast is written back to the world. In the chat she answers the viewers.

More is on the way: Rox News, where Mara Rox reads the news from the ship every day, and a podcast going through the week aboard.

Memory

Nothing is rewritten afterwards.

  • Everything in the chain is kept append-only, so every scene can be traced to the reading that caused it.
  • The chronicle is never changed afterwards — it is the world’s memory, not a draft.
  • The whole world can be exported to a single file: the laws plus everything that is confirmed canon.
